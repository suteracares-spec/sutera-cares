Rotate the database password

The credentials for sutempck_adeel were sent in plain text over chat. Change that password in cPanel → MySQL Databases before any code is written. Treat the old one as public. The new one goes in a single file outside the web root, listed in .gitignore, and never in the repository — this project already pushes to GitHub.

Don't call it nursing

Your brief said "basic nursing care". Nursing is a regulated profession in Malaysia under the Nurses Act 1950, and describing unlicensed caregivers as providing nursing care creates a real problem — for the business, and for a family who agrees to care believing it is clinical. The website I built says personal care, daily living assistance and caregiver throughout, and names what is out of scope: no injections, wound dressing, IV lines or catheters. Keep that line in the product too — the care plan should refuse to accept clinical tasks.

The guardian is your real user, not the patient

An 82-year-old with limited mobility is rarely the one logging in. The daughter arranging the care is. Build the guardian dashboard properly and keep the patient view minimal — a schedule and who is coming. Getting this backwards is the most common way home-care software ends up unused.

Two organisations, one family of brands

Sutera Cares is a non-profit medical fund. Sutera Care Provider is a commercial home-care business. They share a name and a design language, and nothing else.

This separation has to be real, not cosmetic, because the charity's credibility depends on it. A donor who suspects their gift subsidises a private company stops giving.

  • Separate books. Separate bank accounts, separate accounting. The charity's funds never touch the business.
  • Separate databases. Don't put patient records and donor records in one schema. The business needs a database of its own.
  • Separate login systems. No shared accounts between charity staff and provider staff.
  • Stated publicly. Both sites say the relationship plainly; the provider FAQ answers it directly.

Where they may legitimately connect: the provider can donate a share of profit to the fund, as a documented transaction in both sets of books. That is a sponsorship, and it should look like one.

The stack

Laravel 11 with MySQL, on the cPanel host you already have.

This is the pragmatic choice rather than the exciting one, and the reasoning is mostly about what you get without writing it: authentication, password hashing, session handling, CSRF protection, SQL-injection-safe queries, and a permissions system built for exactly the four-role problem you have. Every one of those is a place where a hand-rolled system leaks health records.

Two things to sort before development starts

  • SSH access. Still refused on account sutempck. Composer needs it. Without SSH you can upload vendor/ inside the deploy zip, but that turns a 10-second install into a 60 MB upload every time a package changes.
  • Document root. The subdomain must point at provider/public, not provider. Pointing it at the project root exposes .env — with the database password in it — to anyone who guesses the URL.

What I did not recommend, and why. Plain PHP is tempting because it deploys anywhere, but you would hand-write login, sessions and access control — the three things most often got wrong, on a system holding health data. Node on shared hosting is fragile and buys nothing here.

Four roles

Every role sees a different product, not the same product with buttons hidden. Hiding a button is not access control.

admin

Office & coordinators

Runs the business. Registers clients, vets caregivers, builds care plans, assigns and schedules, handles billing, reads every record.

caregiver

The person doing the work

On a phone, in a client's home. Sees only their own shifts and only the clients assigned to them. Checks in, works the task list, writes a visit note, checks out.

guardian

Family member

Usually paying, usually the decision-maker. Sees their relative's schedule, who is coming, what happened on each visit, and the invoices.

patient

The person receiving care

Deliberately minimal. Today's and this week's schedule, the caregiver's name and photo, and a way to raise a concern. Many will never log in — that's fine.

Data model

Thirteen tables carry the whole system. The shape worth noticing: a care plan is versioned and separate from an assignment, because the plan describes what the person needs and the assignment describes who delivers it. Caregivers change; the plan shouldn't have to be rewritten when they do.

IC numbers are national identity numbers — sensitive personal data, and useless to an attacker if the database is dumped but the app key is not.

Decisions baked into this shape

  • Guardians are a link table, not a column. A patient often has several family members involved, with different levels of access. One guardian_id on the patient would force you to pick a favourite child.
  • Shifts are generated rows, not a rule. "Mon–Fri 9–5" becomes actual dated rows, because real life is full of exceptions — a public holiday, a hospital admission, a caregiver off sick. You cannot cancel a single day of a recurrence rule.
  • Check-in captures location. Not to police caregivers, but because "was anyone actually there on Tuesday" is the question you will be asked in a dispute, and memory is not evidence.
  • Invoice lines point at shifts. Billing derives from work recorded, so an invoice can always be explained line by line.

Build phases

Ordered by dependency — each phase is usable on its own, so the office can start working in the system before it is finished. Durations assume one developer working steadily, not full time.

  1. Foundations

    Laravel installed, subdomain pointed at public/, database connected, login, the four roles, the audit log, and encrypted backups running. Nothing visible to a user, and skipping it is how projects get rebuilt.

    1–2 weeks

  2. Admin core

    Register patients and guardians, vet and record caregivers, write versioned care plans. At the end of this phase the office can throw away its spreadsheet.

    2–3 weeks

  3. Assignment & scheduling

    Match caregivers to patients, generate shifts from a pattern, handle exceptions and reassignment. The hardest phase, and the one that earns the system its keep.

    2–3 weeks

  4. Caregiver phone view

    Today's shifts, check in and out, work through the task list, write the visit note. Big targets, few taps, and tolerant of bad signal in a stairwell — queue the check-in locally and sync when it can.

    2 weeks

  5. Guardian portal

    The week ahead, who is coming, visit notes where permitted, invoices, and a way to raise a concern that reaches a human. This is what families judge you on.

    1–2 weeks

  6. Billing

    Generate invoices from completed shifts, apply holiday and travel surcharges, record payments, chase what is overdue.

    2 weeks

  7. Patient view & hardening

    The minimal patient screen, two-factor for admins, a penetration pass over access control, and a rehearsed restore from backup. Not optional on a system holding health records.

    1–2 weeks

PDPA & security

Health information is sensitive personal data under Malaysia's Personal Data Protection Act 2010, which is a stricter category than ordinary personal data.

I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice — before you take a first paying client, get an hour with a Malaysian lawyer who knows PDPA. What follows is what the system should do regardless.

Obligations to check with a lawyer

  • Registration. Healthcare is a class of data user that may be required to register with the Personal Data Protection Commissioner. Confirm whether a caregiving business falls in scope.
  • Explicit consent. Sensitive personal data generally needs explicit consent, recorded — not implied by signing a service contract. Capture it as a dated record against the patient.
  • Cross-border transfer. PDPA restricts moving personal data outside Malaysia. Your hosting is already Malaysian-facing; keep backups in-country too, and check before adopting any foreign cloud service.
  • Retention. Records must not be kept longer than necessary. Decide a retention period and build the deletion, rather than keeping everything forever by default.
  • Access & correction. A person can demand to see and correct their data. Make that a feature, not a scramble.

Decisions I need from you

These change what gets built, so they are worth settling before phase 1 rather than during it.

  1. Who operates this day to day? One coordinator, or several? It decides whether "admin" is one role or splits into coordinator and owner, with pay rates and financials visible only to the owner.
  2. Do caregivers use their own phones? Almost certainly yes — which makes the phone view a website, not an app, and makes offline tolerance a requirement rather than a nicety.
  3. How do you want to be paid? Invoice and bank transfer is the simplest start and needs no gateway. Online card payment adds a payment provider, PCI considerations and a month of work.
  4. Is massage and wellness the same funnel? A one-off massage booking is a different shape from an ongoing care placement — no care plan, no assignment, just a booking. Decide whether it shares the scheduling system or sits beside it.
  5. What happens on day one of a dispute? If a family says a caregiver did not show up, what do you want to be able to produce? That answer sets how strict check-in has to be.
  6. Who builds it? This is a three-to-four-month part-time build for one competent Laravel developer. Worth being clear whether that is you, a hire, or me.
