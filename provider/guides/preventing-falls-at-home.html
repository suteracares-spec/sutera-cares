Preventing falls at home

5 October 2026 · about 5 minutes to read

A fall can change an older person's life, not just through injury but through lost confidence. The good news is that many falls can be prevented with simple changes to the home and daily habits. Walk through the house with this checklist, ideally together with the person who lives there, and fix the easy things first.

Room by room

Entrance and stairs

  • Keep the doorway and porch clear of shoes, pots and parcels.
  • Mark the edge of any step or raised threshold with a strip of bright tape, so it's easy to see.
  • Make sure stairs are well lit, with switches at both the top and the bottom.
  • Fit sturdy handrails on both sides of the stairs if you can, and check they're firmly fixed.
  • Never leave things on the stairs to "take up later".
  • If stairs have become difficult, consider moving the bedroom downstairs.

Living room

  • Remove loose rugs and mats, or secure them firmly. Tape down curling edges.
  • Keep cables and extension leads along the walls, out of walkways.
  • Arrange furniture so there is a clear, wide path through the room.
  • Choose a firm chair with arms, at a height that makes it easy to stand up from.
  • Keep often-used things, such as the phone, glasses and remote control, within easy reach.

Bedroom

  • Put a lamp or touch light within reach of the bed, and use a night light on the route to the toilet.
  • Check the bed height: feet should rest flat on the floor when sitting on the edge.
  • Sit on the edge of the bed for a moment before standing, especially at night. Getting up too quickly can cause dizziness.
  • Keep a phone by the bed.
  • If night-time trips to the toilet are frequent or rushed, a bedside commode may be safer.

Bathroom

Many Malaysian bathrooms are wet-floor style, with the shower over a tiled floor that stays damp much of the day. This makes the bathroom the riskiest room in many homes, so it deserves the most attention.

  • Lay non-slip mats inside the shower area and outside the bathroom door. Choose ones with rubber backing that grip, and replace them when they wear.
  • Fit grab bars by the toilet and in the shower, screwed into the wall. Towel rails and soap holders are not strong enough to hold someone's weight.
  • Use a shower chair or stool so bathing can be done sitting down.
  • Keep the floor as dry as possible. A floor squeegee after each shower helps, and good ventilation helps it dry faster.
  • Consider a raised toilet seat if getting up is difficult.
  • Keep soap, shampoo and towels within reach so nobody has to stretch or bend.
  • Leave a light on, or fit a night light, so the bathroom is never entered in the dark.
  • Think about whether the door lock is needed. If someone falls inside, family should be able to get in.

Kitchen

  • Store everyday items between waist and shoulder height, to avoid step stools and bending low.
  • Wipe up spills straight away.

Beyond the house

Footwear

  • Wear well-fitting shoes or sandals with thin, non-slip soles and a back strap, indoors as well as outdoors.
  • Avoid loose slippers, walking in socks, and going barefoot on smooth or wet floors.

Eyesight

  • Have eyes checked regularly, and keep glasses clean and to hand.
  • Take extra care with varifocal or bifocal glasses on stairs and kerbs.

Medicines

  • Ask the doctor or pharmacist to review all medicines, especially if your relative takes several. Some can cause dizziness, drowsiness or low blood pressure on standing.
  • Mention any dizziness, unsteadiness or recent near-misses at the appointment.

Staying active

  • Regular movement keeps legs strong and balance steady. Short daily walks, gentle exercise classes or tai chi can all help.
  • Ask the doctor or a physiotherapist which exercises are safe, and whether a walking aid is needed.
  • Drink enough through the day. Dehydration can cause dizziness, particularly in hot weather.

If a fall happens

  • Don't rush to get up, and don't pull them up. Stay calm and take a moment.
  • Check for injury: pain, bleeding, a limb at an odd angle, or being unable to put weight on a leg.
  • If they're unhurt and feel able, they can roll onto their side, get onto hands and knees, and use a sturdy chair to slowly get up, then sit and rest.
  • If they can't get up, keep them warm and comfortable on the floor and call for help.
  • Call 999 if they are hurt, can't get up, are confused, or have hit their head.
  • Tell the doctor about any fall, even if it seems fine. A fall can be a sign of an underlying problem, and some injuries only show later.

Someone living alone should have a way to call for help, such as a mobile phone kept on them or an alarm, and an agreed daily check-in with family or a neighbour.

A caregiver can help with the riskiest moments of the day, such as showering, getting up at night and walking outdoors, and can point out hazards in the home. They work alongside the doctor and physiotherapist, not in place of them.
