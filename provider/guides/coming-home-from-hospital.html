Coming home from hospital: a checklist

5 October 2026 · about 5 minutes to read

Coming home is good news, but the first days after a hospital stay are often when things go wrong: a missed tablet, a fall on the way to the toilet, a follow-up appointment nobody wrote down. A little preparation makes a big difference. Use this checklist to plan ahead, and adapt it to your relative's situation.

Before discharge

Ask the ward team

  • For a written discharge summary that explains the diagnosis, what was done in hospital and what to watch for.
  • For a full medication list: what is new, what has stopped, what has changed dose, and when each one should be taken.
  • For the dates of follow-up appointments, blood tests or scans, and who to call with questions.
  • Whether any clinical care will be needed at home, such as wound dressings, injections or catheter care, and who will provide it. These tasks need a nurse.
  • What your relative can and can't do yet: walking, climbing stairs, bathing, lifting, eating normally.

Ask the physiotherapist or occupational therapist

  • Which exercises should be done at home, how often, and whether they can show a family member.
  • Which walking aid is needed, and the correct height for it.
  • What equipment would help: a commode, shower chair, raised toilet seat, wheelchair or grab rails.

Prepare the home

  • Arrange equipment before the day, not after. Many items can be rented if they're only needed for a few weeks.
  • If stairs are difficult, set up a bedroom on the ground floor, close to a toilet if possible.
  • Clear walkways, remove loose rugs and make sure there is good lighting, including a night light on the way to the toilet.
  • Stock the kitchen with simple, easy-to-prepare food and drinks.
  • Decide who will be at home for the first few days and nights, and write down a plan.

Discharge day

  • Bring comfortable clothes and non-slip shoes for the journey home.
  • Collect the discharge summary, medicines and any letters before you leave the ward. Check the medicines match the list.
  • Allow for waiting. Discharges often take longer than expected, especially waiting for medicines from the pharmacy.
  • Plan the car journey: someone to help with getting in and out, and a wheelchair if walking any distance is hard.
  • Once home, let your relative rest. Expect them to be tired for some days.

The first two weeks

Medicines

  • Use a weekly pill organiser, filled from the current list. Throw out or put away old medicines that have been stopped, so they don't get mixed up.
  • Set alarms or reminders for each dose, and keep a simple chart of what was taken.
  • If something seems wrong, such as dizziness, drowsiness or a rash, ask the doctor or pharmacist before stopping or changing anything.

Warning signs

Ask the ward which signs matter for your relative's condition. In general, contact the doctor promptly if you notice:

  • A fever, new confusion or unusual sleepiness.
  • Increasing pain, swelling, or redness or discharge around a wound.
  • Not eating or drinking, vomiting, or not passing urine.
  • New breathlessness, or a fall.

Call 999 straight away for chest pain, severe difficulty breathing, signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred speech), collapse, heavy bleeding, or if you can't wake them properly.

Eating, drinking and toileting

  • Offer small, frequent meals and drinks through the day. Appetite often takes time to return.
  • Keep water within reach and gently encourage it, especially in hot weather.
  • Make the route to the toilet safe and easy, with a commode or urinal by the bed at night if needed.
  • Watch for constipation, which is common after a hospital stay and with some pain medicines.

Skin and pressure areas

  • If your relative spends a lot of time in bed or a chair, help them change position regularly.
  • Check heels, hips, the base of the spine and elbows for redness that doesn't fade. Tell the doctor or nurse if you see it.
  • Keep skin clean and dry, and bedding smooth.

Appointments and recovery

  • Put every follow-up date on a calendar everyone can see, and arrange transport and someone to go along.
  • Bring the discharge summary and medication list to each appointment, and write down questions beforehand.
  • Keep up the exercises from the physio. Little and often is better than nothing.

Looking after the family carer

  • Share the load. Make a rota with siblings, relatives or neighbours, even for small tasks like shopping.
  • Sleep matters. If nights are broken, arrange for someone else to cover some of them.
  • Accept help when it's offered, and be specific about what would help most.
  • Watch for signs you're running on empty: irritability, exhaustion, feeling low. Talk to your own doctor if it doesn't lift.

Some families bring in a caregiver for the first few weeks, to help with bathing, mobility, meals, medication reminders and getting to appointments, while the family catches its breath. Clinical tasks still need a nurse.
