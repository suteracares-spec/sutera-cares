Caregiver, maid or nursing home? Choosing the right help

5 October 2026 · about 5 minutes to read

When a parent or grandparent starts needing more help, most families in the Klang Valley weigh up the same few options. Each one suits a different situation, and none is right for everyone. This guide sets out what each option is good for, where it falls short, and what to check before you commit.

A domestic helper (maid)

What it suits

  • Households that mainly need help with cooking, cleaning, laundry and keeping someone company.
  • An older person who is still fairly independent with washing, dressing and walking.
  • Families who want someone living in the home over the long term.

What it doesn't suit

  • Someone who needs regular hands-on personal care, such as help in the shower, toileting or transfers from bed to chair. Most helpers are hired for housework and are not trained in safe moving and handling.
  • Situations where you need cover quickly, or only for a few weeks.

Things to consider

Hiring a foreign domestic helper normally goes through a licensed agency and involves permits, a levy and other paperwork, which can take time. You are the employer, so days off, accommodation, wellbeing and replacement if things don't work out are your responsibility. If your relative's needs grow, a helper's role may need topping up with trained support.

A trained home caregiver

What it suits

  • Someone who wants to stay at home but needs help with personal care, mobility, meals, medication reminders or getting to appointments.
  • Families who need flexible hours: a few hours a day, full days, overnight, or live-in.
  • Short-term needs, such as the weeks after a hospital stay, or giving a family carer a break.

What it doesn't suit

  • Clinical care. Non-medical caregivers do not give injections, dress wounds, or manage IV lines, catheters or feeding tubes.
  • Very tight budgets where round-the-clock cover is needed for years. Long hours of trained care add up, and a mix of family, helper and caregiver may work better.

Things to consider

Ask the provider how caregivers are screened and trained, who covers if the regular caregiver is sick, and how the family is kept informed. At Sutera, every placement starts with a home assessment and a written care plan, and the family can see visit notes after each visit.

A nursing home or care centre

What it suits

  • Someone who needs supervision day and night and can no longer be kept safe at home, even with help.
  • Families who cannot realistically provide or organise care at home.
  • People who would enjoy, or at least accept, living alongside others with staff on hand.

What it doesn't suit

  • Someone who is strongly attached to their home, routines and neighbourhood, or who becomes distressed by change, as many people living with dementia do.
  • Families wanting one-to-one attention; staff look after several residents at once.

Things to consider

Care homes in Malaysia should be properly licensed or registered. Ask to see the licence and check it with the relevant authority. Always visit in person, ideally more than once and at different times of day. Look at cleanliness, how staff speak to residents, whether residents are up and engaged, and how medicines and meals are handled.

Home nursing

What it suits

  • Clinical needs at home: wound care, injections, catheter or stoma care, tube feeding, or monitoring after surgery.
  • Short courses of treatment that would otherwise mean extra time in hospital.

What it doesn't suit

Day-to-day help and companionship. Nursing visits are usually short and focused on the clinical task. Many families combine a nurse's visits with a caregiver who helps with everything else.

Things to consider

Use a licensed nursing provider with registered nurses, and ask the hospital team or your doctor which tasks genuinely need a nurse. If you are unsure whether a need is medical or not, ask before you arrange care, not after.

Questions to ask before you decide

  • What does your relative want? Have they been part of the conversation?
  • What help is needed today, and what is likely to be needed in six months?
  • Are any of the needs clinical? If so, who will meet them?
  • Is someone needed at night, or only during the day?
  • Who in the family will coordinate care, and how much time do they really have?
  • What can the family afford month after month, not just for the first few weeks?
  • What happens if the main carer, paid or unpaid, falls ill or needs a break?
  • Is the home safe, or does it need changes such as grab bars or a ground-floor bedroom?

An honest note

Many families end up with a mix: a helper for the household, a caregiver for personal care or busy periods, and a nurse for specific clinical tasks. That is often the most practical answer. What matters is being clear about who does what. Clinical needs should always be met by a licensed nursing provider, and a good caregiving service will tell you plainly when something is outside what it can safely do.
